## D1 — The LLM plans and explains; code computes every number

- **Decision:** The LLM produces (1) a query plan and (2) a narrative explanation. Scores, ranks and percentages come only from deterministic Python.
- **Why:** Every number stays auditable and reproducible. This also defeats prompt injection aimed at the numbers.
- **Rejected:** Letting the LLM read raw data and compute or estimate answers.

## D2 — Plan → execute → explain, not a free-form tool loop

- **Decision:** Each turn makes two LLM calls: a planner (`QueryPlan`) and an explainer (`Explanation`), with one deterministic executor between them.
- **Why:**
  - Every target question maps to one operation (rank, compare, stat, explain).
  - A single plan can be evaluated field by field.
  - Two calls fit Groq's 8K tokens/min.
  - No looping or tool-argument drift.
- **Trade-off:** Compound questions are served by the richer operation. For example, *compare* already includes per-hazard breakdowns.

## D3 — Flat, strict JSON schemas + local validation + grounding check

- **Decision:**
  - Schemas are flat. Every field is required, and fields that may not apply are nullable; `additionalProperties: false`.
  - Output is always validated with Pydantic. On failure there is one retry with the errors, then a clear error answer. A provider rejecting its own JSON (Groq strict mode returns HTTP 400) counts as invalid output, not as an outage.
  - Every number in the explanation must appear in the computed result or the question. If not: retry once, then use a deterministic template.
- **Why:** This is the shape Groq's strict mode requires, and Gemini accepts it, so one schema serves both providers. The grounding check enforces D1 on the narrative.

## D4 — Assumptions, uncertainty, sources and time window are generated by code

- **Decision:** The answer assembler builds these sections from the plan, the result and `source_status`. They are generated from config, so they cannot go stale.
- **Why:** They appear in every answer by construction, not because a prompt asked for them.

## D5 — Groq `openai/gpt-oss-20b`, Gemini fallback, pinned LiteLLM

- **Decision:**
  - All LLM calls go through LiteLLM.
  - Primary: Groq `openai/gpt-oss-20b`. Fallback: Gemini `gemini-3.8-flash`. Model IDs come from env.
  - A short rate limit (≤ 12 s) is waited out once on Groq. Longer limits, 5xx errors and timeouts go to Gemini.
- **Why:** Groq's free tier allows 8K tokens/min, which a busy demo hits quickly; Gemini absorbs the overflow.
- **Security:** LiteLLM 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 were malicious PyPI releases (2026-03-24). The version is pinned, with hashes in `uv.lock`.

## D6 — Two public sources in the core

| Source | Job |
|---|---|
| Open-Meteo archive (ERA5 reanalysis) | Observed weather: exposure indicators and "% of days" stats |
| FEMA National Risk Index (county) | Modeled hazard loss per county |

- **Out of the core (D21):** NWS alerts and Open-Meteo forecast (near-term), and OpenFEMA declarations. The code is recoverable from commit `d9e6f13`.
- **Not used:** NOAA ACIS station data (user decision; the reanalysis caveat is stated instead).

## D7 — NRI building loss rate → our own national percentile

- **Decision:** Use `{HAZARD}_ALRB` (expected annual building loss rate) and rank it against all 3,232 US counties ourselves. Null means "hazard not applicable" and scores 0.
- **Why:**
  - `RISK_SCORE` is 95–100 for every metro hub (Denver 95.2, Cook 99.97, Dallas 99.65), because it scales with the dollar value exposed in the county.
  - The composite `*_ALR_NPCTL` includes agriculture: Miami's cold-wave percentile is 91 because of crop freezes.
  - A building loss rate measures how hazardous the place is for a facility, independent of county size.
- **Note:** Flood uses `IFLD` (Inland Flooding) and `CFLD` (Coastal). NRI version: December 2025.

## D8 — *(removed by D21)* FEMA disaster declarations

- Major-disaster declarations were a third "history" lens. Removed to keep the core to two sources. As a result, hurricane and severe-storm scores now rest on NRI alone, and answers say so.

## D9 — Previous 5 completed years, fetched on demand and cached

- **Decision:**
  - Exposure uses the previous 5 completed calendar years (2021–2025 when run in 2026).
  - The window is computed from today's date, and waits for the ~6-day archive lag in early January.
  - Weather is fetched per (hub, year) only when a question needs it, then cached in SQLite.
  - A completed year is never fetched again; the current year is refreshed after 12 hours.
- **Why:**
  - Nothing is preloaded, and there is no seed at runtime (D21).
  - One hub-year costs about 26 Open-Meteo calls, so per-year caching keeps traffic minimal.
- **Trade-off:**
  - A cold first ranking of all 10 hubs needs about 1,300 calls. The shared rate limiter (400 calls/min, under the free 600/min) makes that take about 3 minutes.
  - `uv run python -m weather_risk.warm` pre-warms the cache before a demo. It's optional.
- **Earlier choice (superseded):** 10 years preloaded as a committed seed snapshot.

## D10 — *(removed by D21)* Separate near-term score

- A 7-day near-term score (forecast + NWS alerts) drove alerts. It's out of the core for now. If alerts return as a bonus, they will fire on exposure-score changes.

## D11 — Fixed-anchor normalization, not min-max

- **Decision:** Each indicator maps to 0–100 through documented anchors (e.g. 15 snow days a year = 100), capped.
- **Why:** A hub's score doesn't move when other hubs are added or removed, so a score change always means a data change.
- **Rejected:** Min-max or rank scaling across the roster.

## D12 — Overall exposure = equal-weight mean of families

- **Decision:** Overall = mean of winter, hurricane, flood, severe storm and heat (user decision). Weights can be overridden per question.
- **Why:** It's the most neutral default without the company's own loss data.
- **Trade-off:** A hub with one extreme hazard (Miami/hurricane) is diluted. Answers always show each hub's top hazard, and hazard-specific questions rank by family.

## D13 — Stat thresholds in config, always printed

- **Decision:** "Snowfall day" means ≥ 0.25 cm (the NWS "measurable" 0.1 in). Every stat answer shows a sensitivity row and flags the threshold when it changes the answer two-fold or more.
- **Why:** The threshold dominates the answer. For Denver 2025 (city-center check during planning) it swung from 2.7% to 12.1%:

  | Threshold | Days | % of 2025 |
  |---|---|---|
  | Any snow > 0 | 44 | 12.1% |
  | ≥ 0.25 cm (measurable) | 31 | 8.5% |
  | ≥ 1 cm | 18 | 4.9% |
  | ≥ 2.5 cm (1 in) | 10 | 2.7% |

  Reanalysis also differs from the airport station (26 measurable-snow days).

## D14 — Async SQLAlchemy + SQLite (aiosqlite)

- **Call-out:** The approved plan chose stdlib `sqlite3` with no ORM. Mid-build the user asked for async SQLAlchemy + SQLite, believing that was the agreed choice. I checked whether stdlib `sqlite3` was *materially* better for this assignment. It isn't, so the store moved to async SQLAlchemy 2.0.
- **Trade-off:**
  - **For SQLAlchemy:** typed models, fits the async FastAPI/httpx stack, and moving to Postgres is a URL change.
  - **Against it:** 2 more dependencies, a bit more code, and aiosqlite gives no real concurrency on SQLite.
- **Detail:** SQLite allows one writer, so the store serializes writes with an async lock rather than hitting "database is locked". WAL mode lets readers run while the API writes.
- **Rejected:** Postgres (no concurrency need; it's the migration path), JSON files (no range queries or transactions), DuckDB (built for analytics).

## D15 — One run script; Docker optional; no cloud deploy yet

- **Decision:** `uv run python scripts/run.py` starts FastAPI (:8000) and Streamlit (:8501) together and stops them together. The Dockerfile runs the same script. There is no Makefile. The cloud host will be chosen later (user decision).

## D16 — Eval: offline replay by default, `--live` for the real LLM

- **Decision:**
  - **Offline:** replays recorded real API responses (`tests/fixtures/recorded/`, exactly the requests the runtime makes), feeds each case's expected plan to the executor, and uses the template explainer. No keys, no network.
  - **`--live`:** runs the real planner and explainer over the same replayed data.
- **Why:** Offline checks the deterministic core and the answer contract on every run. Live measures plan accuracy and grounding. Replayed data keeps expected numbers stable. Recorded responses are test fixtures only, never a runtime dependency (D21).

## D17 — Pragmatic TDD for core logic

- **Decision:** Core logic (scoring, stats, windows, config validation, plan validation, grounding, executor, assembler) is written test-first with hand-computed fixtures. I/O glue is tested alongside, with every external call mocked or replayed.
- **Why:** The numbers are the product, so they get the strongest guarantees.

## D18 — Identity-lite auth

- **Decision:** An allow-listed email gets a signed bearer token with an `analyst` or `admin` role. Conversations belong to their owner.
- **Limitation (documented):** There is no password or magic link. This is not production authentication.

## D19 — Hub roster: 10 representative hubs (an assumption)

- **Decision:** 10 hubs at each metro's main airport logistics zone. Counties were looked up once via the FCC Area API.
  - Midwest (US Census definition): Chicago, Minneapolis–St Paul, Detroit, Kansas City
  - Named in the brief: Denver, Dallas, Houston, Miami
  - Coverage: Atlanta (Southeast), Newark (Northeast)
- **Why:** The brief gives no facility list. Four Midwest hubs make "most exposed in the Midwest" a real ranking. Earlier choice: 15 hubs (narrowed by D21).

## D20 — Calibration after the first real-data run

- **What the first run showed:** Kansas City and Memphis outscored Minneapolis on winter. Yet Minneapolis averages 22.6 days a year at or below −18 °C, against Memphis's 0.1.
- **Root causes, measured in the data:**
  1. ERA5 smooths daily snowfall. The "≥ 15 cm in a day" indicator was 0 at every hub; Minneapolis's largest single day in 10 years was 14.9 cm. Annual totals run ~25–30 % below station normals.
  2. NRI building-loss rates measure structural damage. Winter and heat disrupt hubs through operations, so the modeled lens pulled winter scores toward counties with small building stocks.
- **Changes (in `config/scoring.yaml`):**
  - Winter and heat weigh observed weather 0.75 and NRI 0.25. Other hazards use 0.5 / 0.5.
  - Heavy snow is ≥ 10 cm/day (1.5 days/yr = 100).
  - Snow days (≥ 2.5 cm) score 100 at 15 days a year.
- **Result (2021–2025):** Midwest winter order is Minneapolis 67.7, Chicago 48.8, Kansas City 46.5, Detroit 41.7. Chicago and Kansas City are flagged as a near-tie.
- **Risk acknowledged:** Calibrating after seeing results can bake in expectations. Each change is tied to a measured property of the data, not to a target ranking. All values stay in config, and the sensitivity test reports how fragile any order is.

## D21 — Narrow one-day scope (mid-build rescope)

- **Requested by the user** after M3 was built, to fit the one-day budget.
- **Changes:**
  - Hubs cut from 15 to 10 (D19).
  - Core sources are Open-Meteo ERA5 and FEMA NRI only (D6). NWS, the forecast, OpenFEMA and the near-term score were removed (D8, D10).
  - Exposure uses the previous 5 completed years, fetched on demand and cached (D9). No runtime seed.
  - `hubs.yaml` and `scoring.yaml` are validated at startup by typed Pydantic models; a bad config fails fast with a readable error.
  - Persistence moved to async SQLAlchemy (D14).
  - Alerts and voice stay outside the core until the agent, API, UI and evals work.
- **Kept:** verified FIPS values, the YAML structure, recorded real API fixtures, the source adapters, the TDD approach and the D20 calibration.
- **Lost evidence, stated in answers:** without FEMA declarations, hurricane and severe-storm scores rest on NRI alone.
